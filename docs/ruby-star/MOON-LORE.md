# One Last Dance

## The First Mirror

Deep beneath the Moon's surface lay a small town built inside an enormous crystal cavern.

Its people had always loved music. Every week, they gathered in a great hall where crystal chandeliers illuminated the dance floor. Families danced together, children chased each other between the tables, and the music often continued long after midnight.

The Shadow Queen was a familiar presence at these celebrations. Though she ruled the Moon, she preferred sitting among her people to watching them from a throne.

She especially enjoyed attending with her younger sister.

The two were inseparable. Her sister was a terrible dancer, an excellent cook, and had a habit of talking through every important moment of a performance.

Then, one winter, her sister died.

The Queen stopped attending the dances.

For months, she remained in her palace, surrounded by things her sister had left behind. Her kitchen was particularly difficult to enter. There were still two chairs at the table, two cups in the cupboard, and a small knife her sister had used to peel fruit.

One evening, while sitting alone in the kitchen, the Queen discovered something extraordinary.

By focusing the power of her Amethyst into an ordinary mirror, she could recreate a memory.

The first image she summoned was of her sister sitting at the kitchen table, peeling an apple.

The image was perfect.

Her sister moved the knife carefully beneath the skin, trying to remove the peel in one continuous strip. The Queen remembered watching her do this countless times.

She watched until the peel broke.

Then the memory repeated.

The Queen laughed for the first time in months.

She tried to speak to her sister, but received no response. The figure in the mirror was not alive. It was simply a memory, repeating itself exactly as the Queen remembered it.

Her sister's lips moved, but no words came out.

The Queen realized she could not remember what her sister had been saying that afternoon.

Nevertheless, she kept the mirror.

For the first time since her sister's death, the kitchen no longer felt entirely empty.

## Borrowed Moments

News of the Queen's discovery spread throughout the town.

The first visitor was an elderly man whose wife had died the previous winter.

He arrived carrying a small travel mirror with a broken hinge.

When the Queen asked which memory he wanted to see, he chose an ordinary evening.

His wife had returned home late, believing he was asleep. She tried to remove her boots quietly but accidentally knocked one against the kitchen table.

The Queen placed the mirror before him.

The man's wife appeared in the glass, struggling with her boots.

Before the boot struck the table, the old man began laughing.

He knew exactly what was coming.

He had remembered that evening many times, yet seeing it again somehow made it funnier.

The Queen repaired the mirror's hinge and sent him home with it.

Word spread. People brought the Queen first steps, weddings and voices they hadn’t heard in years. She soon learned what the glass could hold.

A mirror could only reproduce what its owner remembered.

It could not reveal forgotten secrets, recover conversations that had faded from memory, or show what happened outside the owner's awareness.

If someone remembered a face but not a voice, the reflection would remain silent.

If someone remembered an argument differently from how it had occurred, the mirror would reproduce their recollection, not necessarily the truth.

And if a person wished to preserve only a particular part of a memory, the Queen could shorten the reflection to play just that moment.

She never altered someone's memories without their permission.

Before returning each mirror, she always offered the same reminder:

“The glass remembers what you give it. The rest of your life still belongs to you.”

For years, people took comfort in what she gave them.

And she continued watching her sister peel the same apple, every evening.

## The Girl Who Wouldn't Let Go

Among the Queen's visitors was a woman named Nella.

As a child, Nella had learned to dance from her father in the great cavern hall.

Her father was an enthusiastic but hopeless dancer. He counted too quickly, stepped on her feet, and always seemed to forget which direction he was supposed to turn.

He laughed whenever he made a mistake.

Nella used to complain that he was impossible to dance with.

But she never refused when he asked.

One afternoon, after a particularly disastrous dance, they argued.

The heel of Nella's favorite shoe had come loose. Her father insisted on repairing it before she walked home.

Nella, who was growing tired of being treated like a child, told him she could look after her own shoes.

They exchanged a few sharp words.

She left the hall angry.

Later that evening, she found a little paper bag of shoe nails tucked into her coat pocket.

Her father had slipped them inside while she wasn't looking.

She meant to tease him about it the next morning.

She put the nails in her shoe bag at the hall in case the heel gave way again.

They did see each other again, many times over the following years. The argument was forgotten, replaced by other conversations and ordinary days.

Then, years later, her father died.

While sorting through the dance hall's old storage cupboard, Nella discovered the same bag of nails.

That was when she began crying.

Not because she remembered their argument.

But because she suddenly remembered all the little things her father had done without ever asking for thanks.

She brought a mirror to the Queen.

Nella asked to relive the afternoon they had danced together.

At first, the reflection included everything: her father's clumsy steps, their laughter, his concern about her shoe, and the argument that followed.

Nella asked the Queen to remove the argument.

The Queen obliged.

A few weeks later, Nella returned.

She asked to shorten the memory again.

Then again.

Eventually, the mirror showed only a few seconds.

Her father stood at the edge of the dance floor.

He smiled and raised his hand, inviting her to join him.

Then the memory repeated.

He smiled.

He raised his hand.

And he waited.

Nella placed the mirror beside her bed so that she could see her father greeting her every morning.

Later, she began bringing it to the dance hall.

She liked imagining that he was still waiting for their next dance.

## The Hall of Reflections

Nella was not alone.

People began bringing their mirrors to the weekly dances. At first they left them beside the tables. Then came requests to turn the music down and complaints about dancers crossing in front of the glass. The hall keeper made room for chairs until the floor belonged to people sitting down. He kept winding the music box. After a while, he was winding it for himself.

One night, he moved a chair to clear a small space on the floor.

Nella immediately asked him to put it back.

From that particular angle, her father appeared to be waving directly toward the entrance.

The keeper apologized and returned the chair.

He sat beside her for a while.

He had known Nella's father for decades. In fact, he recognized the shirt the man was wearing in the reflection.

He had lent it to him years ago.

The keeper was about to tell Nella the story when her father's reflection raised its hand again.

Nella raised hers in response.

The keeper fell silent.

He watched her repeat the gesture three times.

Then he went to turn off the music.

Nobody noticed.

That evening, the Queen visited the hall for the first time in years.

She stood in the doorway and looked across the room.

Dozens of mirrors shimmered in the darkness.

Inside them, people laughed, danced, embraced, and spoke to companions who could no longer answer.

Outside the glass, the hall was almost completely still.

She recognized the reflections. She had made most of them herself. She returned to the kitchen without saying a word.

## The Queen's Reflection

That night, the Queen sat before the mirror in her kitchen.

Her sister appeared at the table, peeling an apple.

The Queen watched the knife move.

Her sister's lips formed words that the Queen could not hear.

She had replayed the memory thousands of times.

She knew exactly where the apple peel would break.

She knew the movement of her sister's hands, the slight tilt of her head, and the smile that came just before the memory restarted.

But she still did not know what her sister was saying.

No matter how many times she watched, the missing words never returned.

For years, she had convinced herself that one day she might remember.

That if she watched carefully enough, she would notice something she had missed.

But the mirror could only offer what she already possessed.

The Queen reached toward the glass.

Her sister reached for another apple.

The memory began again.

This time, the Queen turned the mirror face down before the peel could break.

She remained sitting there for a long while.

Then she stood, took a real apple from the bowl, and tried to peel it herself.

She had watched her sister do it so many times that she thought it would be easy.

The peel broke almost immediately.

The Queen tried again.

And again.

Finally, she set down the knife and laughed at herself.

Her sister would have found it hilarious.

For the first time in years, the Queen remembered her sister without looking into the mirror.

The following morning, the Queen considered covering every mirror in the town.

She imagined the dance hall filled with music again, and her people finally returning to the lives waiting outside their reflections.

But she remembered the old man laughing as his wife's boot struck the table.

She remembered the mother who had wept with happiness at seeing her son's first steps.

And she remembered how much comfort her own mirror had given her.

The mirrors were not cruel.

They had never promised that the people inside them were still alive.

Perhaps the mistake was expecting them to provide something they could not.

The Queen left her mirror on the kitchen table.

She did not cover it.

She simply opened the curtains and went outside.

There was a dance scheduled for the following evening.

She decided she would attend.

## One Last Dance

The next evening, the hall keeper began preparing for the weekly dance.

As usual, he arranged chairs for the mirrors.

As usual, Nella arrived carrying her father's reflection.

She placed it in its usual position and watched him raise his hand.

The keeper approached with a question.

“Was your father a good dancer?” asked the keeper.

Nella looked surprised.

Then she laughed.

“Terrible. Absolutely terrible,” said Nella.

“I remember,” said the keeper.

“He stepped on my feet six times in one dance.”

She glanced at the mirror.

“Back when the glass still showed the whole dance, I asked the Queen to cut it off at five. Thought I'd be generous.”

The keeper chuckled.

He told her about the shirt her father was wearing.

Years ago, Nella's father had borrowed it for a celebration because he had spilled soup on his own clothes.

He had promised to return it the next day.

Instead, he returned it three weeks later, freshly washed, with one sleeve accidentally dyed pink.

Nella burst out laughing.

She had never heard that story.

Her father had been gone for years, yet she had just learned something new about him.

The keeper told her another story.

Then she told him one.

They talked about the old dances, the broken coat rack, and the time her father had accidentally led three couples into the same corner.

Eventually, Nella looked toward the mirror.

Her father raised his hand.

She realized she had spent years watching him invite her to dance.

But she had never once accepted.

She stood up and gently turned the mirror away from the floor.

Then she asked the keeper to move the chairs.

He looked confused.

“All of them?” asked the keeper.

“Not all. Just enough for two people,” said Nella.

They cleared a little space.

The keeper went to the old music box and began winding it.

The handle caught on the third turn, as it always did.

Nella reached over, turned it backward slightly, and released the mechanism without looking.

The keeper smiled.

“You've done that before,” said the keeper.

“My father taught me,” said Nella.

The music began.

Nella offered him her hand.

They stepped onto the empty floor.

She led with the wrong foot.

The keeper stumbled.

For a moment, they stared at each other.

Then Nella began laughing so hard that she could barely stand.

“That's how he taught me!” said Nella.

The keeper laughed too.

They tried again.

A few people looked up from their mirrors.

Someone moved a chair out of the way.

Then another.

An elderly couple stood and joined them.

A young woman who had never learned the old dances asked someone to teach her.

Slowly, the floor began filling.

Nobody ordered the mirrors removed.

Nobody covered them or asked anyone to forget.

They simply made room for something else.

When the Shadow Queen arrived, she found the hall alive with music for the first time in years.

She stood in the doorway, watching.

Some people were still sitting beside their reflections.

Others had left their mirrors on the tables while they danced.

Nella's mirror rested against a chair near the wall.

Her father was still waving.

But Nella was no longer watching him.

She was too busy trying to teach the hall keeper the wrong steps.

The Queen smiled.

For the first time since her sister's death, she joined the dance.

## A Place for Yesterday

One evening, the Queen set out two spoons for tea by habit. Her sister’s mirror was still on the table. She left it facing the wall and spoke toward the empty chair.

“You know, I still can’t peel an apple properly.”

She could almost hear her sister laugh. Outside, music drifted through the cavern. The Queen put one spoon back in the drawer and took her coat.

There was a dance tonight.

And this time, she intended to stay until the end.
