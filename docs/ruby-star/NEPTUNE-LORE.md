# The Song That Found You

## The Voice Beneath the Waves

Deep beneath Neptune's endless ocean lay a kingdom of glowing coral, ancient underwater cities, and magnificent creatures that drifted through the darkness.

Whales sang across the caverns and schools of fish moved like tiny bells. People came from all over to hear the Sea Chanter. Her Sapphire carried her voice through the water, where it healed wounded creatures and calmed currents. Few people asked what she wanted to say when she wasn’t singing.

Until one evening, someone answered her song.

She sang three notes to herself over an abandoned reef. Three metallic notes answered from far below. The last was completely wrong. She tried something harder and frightened a school of fish laughing at the reply. The bells answered every evening. He never could keep up, but he always tried.

Eventually, curiosity overcame her.

She followed the sound deep into the ocean, toward an old maintenance station built beside an enormous underwater trench.

Inside, a young engineer was striking a set of resonance bells with a metal wrench.

He looked up when she appeared at the station's open water chamber.

The wrench slipped from his hand.

It struck every bell on its way to the floor.

A spectacularly unpleasant noise echoed through the station.

The engineer turned bright red.

“So you're the one who's been ruining my songs,” said the Chanter.

“I prefer to think of it as improving them,” said the engineer.

“You've been playing the last note wrong for twelve days,” said the Chanter.

“And yet you keep coming back,” said the engineer.

The Chanter opened her mouth to reply.

Then closed it.

For once, someone had managed to leave her speechless.

His name was Cael.

And he was smiling.

## An Unusual Duet

Cael kept Neptune’s old acoustic network working. Most people had moved on to newer equipment. He still cleared coral from the resonance bells so isolated settlements could send messages through the currents. The Chanter started visiting, supposedly to learn how the bells worked. Cael pretended to believe her.

On her first visit, he sent a repair code from the smallest bell and waited. A minute later, an answering tone came from a settlement across the trench. Their newer equipment had been out for weeks. The Chanter asked how he could tell so much from a single note. He showed her the old chart by his desk, where somebody had penciled in corrections beside the printed codes.

“I'm an engineer, not a musician,” said Cael.

“That much is obvious,” said the Chanter.

“You're welcome to teach me,” said Cael.

“Are you sure? It could take years,” said the Chanter.

“I've got time,” said Cael.

So she began teaching him.

Cael could hear the slightest fault in a bell but couldn’t count four beats. Between lessons, he asked what she liked to eat and what she did when she didn’t feel like singing. Nobody had asked her that before.

On evenings when she arrived tired from a performance, he put his tools down and let her talk. Once she sat with him through an entire inspection without singing a note. He asked if she wanted to come back the next day.

She took him to gardens where flowers opened to a certain note. He tried to cook for her, with a recipe that involved boiling everything until it was the same shade of blue. Then he made her a music box. The last note always came out wrong. She kept it beside her bed.

One evening, they visited a shallow reef overlooking the distant lights of the underwater capital.

Bioluminescent creatures drifted around them like stars.

Cael had brought a small diving lantern.

The Chanter asked why he needed it when the entire reef was glowing.

“In case you get lost,” said Cael.

“I'm the Sea Chanter. I know this ocean better than anyone,” said the Chanter.

“Then I'll use it when I get lost,” said Cael.

They watched the lights drift into deeper water. Cael took her hand and pointed to a ridge where luminous whales passed above the trench. He wanted to build an observatory there, with windows facing the migration and walls that carried sound just right.

“You'd build an entire observatory just to hear me sing?” asked the Chanter.

“Not just to hear you sing,” said Cael.

He looked at her.

“I'd like somewhere we could be together when you don't feel like singing, too.”

The Chanter leaned against his shoulder.

For once, she didn't know what to say.

And she didn't need to.

## The Broken Message

Almost two years later, an earthquake damaged the old resonance stations. Cael found bells that were still echoing hours after the original sound had faded. A Sapphire-strength song could build through the broken network until it shook whole settlements apart. He sent a warning through the bells.

“Please stop singing through the network. The resonance is unstable. I can't bear to hear what it's doing to the walls. Until I can fix it, please, no more songs,” said Cael.

What reached the Chanter was very different.

“Please stop singing...”

“...I can't bear to hear...”

“...no more songs.”

She played the fragments again and tried to answer. No reply came. Cael had already descended to the central resonance chamber. A second tremor brought down the passage behind his vessel. He reached the emergency shelter near the unfinished observatory, where he had air and a few weeks of supplies.

Above him, the Chanter followed the damaged beacons to the trench. The current was too fierce and the passage was blocked. She called his name. Only the ocean answered.

Days passed.

Then weeks.

The search crews turned back at the same break in the passage. His vessel had left the station before anyone thought to ask where he was going. On the worst nights she wondered if he had chosen to go. She had only those three scraps of his voice to go on.

But every evening, when the ocean grew quiet, she found herself listening for three familiar notes.

They never came.

## The Silence Between Them

She still sang for the cities and healed wounded creatures, away from the old bells. Sometimes she went back to the shallow reef. The little lights still drifted with the current. She tried to be angry with Cael. It was easier than admitting she missed him.

In the trench he repaired conduits, regulators and whatever bells he could reach. When he needed a rest, he sat at the table he had fitted beside the unfinished observatory’s window. There were two chairs and a bell tuned to the first tune he had played for her. He had meant it as a surprise. He wondered if any of his messages had reached her.

After weeks of repairs, the central resonance chamber finally began functioning again.

But its connection to the upper network remained damaged.

Cael could send only a very weak signal.

He sent emergency codes, his name and the shelter’s coordinates. Nothing came back.

He checked the chart above the workbench. It assumed a receiving station would be listening for a code. Somewhere above him the Chanter might still be listening too. He reached for the striker beside the table.

Finally, exhausted and nearly out of ideas, he struck the bell three times.

The same three notes he had played on the evening they first met.

The last note was wrong.

He knew it was wrong.

He had always known.

But he played it anyway.

## The Song That Found You

High above the trench, the Sea Chanter was tending to an injured whale.

She had been singing softly, using the Sapphire to ease the creature's pain.

Then something interrupted her.

Three faint metallic notes drifted through the water.

She stopped. The last note was wrong. She followed it down, pausing whenever it disappeared into the currents.

At last, she reached the collapsed passage leading to the ancient resonance chamber.

There, beneath enormous slabs of broken stone, she discovered a faint blue light.

Someone was still inside.

The Chanter placed her hand against the rock.

She could feel the vibrations of the bell beyond it.

When Cael didn’t answer her call, she sang the melody she’d taught him. Inside the shelter he dropped his tools and ran to the passage.

“You're here?” asked Cael.

She laughed, then began crying.

Using the Sapphire, she guided a gentle current through the cracks in the stone, carefully clearing enough debris to open the passage without damaging the unstable chamber.

The first slab shifted and caught on a strut. Cael called through the gap for her to stop. He braced the strut from his side while she held the current steady. When he called again, she eased the stone past him.

When the opening was wide enough, Cael stepped through.

He had lost weight, and his diving suit was mostly patches. He was smiling.

“You absolute idiot!” said the Chanter.

Cael blinked.

“I missed you too,” said Cael.

She threw her arms around him.

“I thought you weren't answering because you were angry,” said Cael.

“I was angry!” said the Chanter.

“Why?” asked Cael.

“You told me you couldn't bear to hear me sing!” said the Chanter.

She repeated the scraps of the warning she had heard. He told her what the bells had done to the rest of it.

“So you weren't telling me to stop singing because you were tired of me?” asked the Chanter.

“I was telling you to stop singing because you might accidentally destroy three cities,” said Cael.

She stared at him.

“You could have explained that!” said the Chanter.

“I tried!” said Cael.

“Your message was terrible!” said the Chanter.

“The equipment was broken!” said Cael.

“You're the engineer!” said the Chanter.

Cael opened his mouth.

Then closed it.

“That's actually a fair point,” said Cael.

Their laughter echoed through the damaged chamber. After a while, Cael took her hand.

“There's something I want to show you,” said Cael.

He led her through a narrow corridor toward a small door.

Above it hung a crooked sign.

“For the Sea Chanter.”

He opened the door.

Beyond it stood the observatory he had promised her.

Its enormous windows overlooked the deep ocean, where thousands of luminous creatures drifted through the darkness.

Two chairs sat beside a little table.

A resonance bell hung above them.

The room was unfinished in places, and several tools were scattered across the floor.

But it was beautiful.

“You built it.”

“I was going to surprise you,” Cael said, looking around at the damage. “The earthquake ruined the presentation.”

A luminous whale passed the window. She watched it for a moment, then reached for his hand.

## The Observatory of Two Voices

It took weeks to put the old network right. The Chanter steadied the currents while crews from nearby settlements repaired the conduits. At the observatory’s opening, she was nervous in a way she’d never been before a crowd.

“What if they don’t like it?”

“Sing something you like,” Cael said.

She chose the little melody from the reef. This time the old bells carried it safely. Cael looked at the gauges, then took his hands off the controls and listened. A bell across the trench answered with the same three notes.

Later, after the guests left, they sat beside the window with two cups of tea. Hers was far too sweet. His was almost entirely hot water.

Then Cael stood and walked toward the little resonance bell above their table.

He picked up the striker.

Three familiar notes rang through the room.

The last one was wrong.

The Chanter looked up.

“You still haven't learned how to play that properly,” said the Chanter.

“I've had months to practice,” said Cael.

“And you're still getting the last note wrong,” said the Chanter.

“I know,” said Cael.

She tilted her head.

“Then why don't you fix it?” asked the Chanter.

Cael smiled.

“Because that's the part where you always answer,” said Cael.

She crossed the room and sang the missing note softly against his shoulder.
