# The Space Between Us

## Two Stars, One Station

Somewhere between two stars floated a station that had spent the last seven hundred years arguing with itself.

On one side shone an enormous coral-colored star, bathing the station in golden warmth. On the other glowed a smaller blue star, casting a beautiful, cold light across the hull.

Four chambers drew power from both stars. People once shared their markets and music until a magnetic surge tore through the junction. The emergency barriers came down and stayed down. They called it the Great Separation.

The warm side became known as Amberward.

Amberward rang in the day with drums and served meals hot enough to melt utensils. Its history books blamed Azureward for sabotaging the station out of jealousy.

Azureward preferred quiet streets, a single morning bell and a schedule for everything. Its records blamed Amberward for trying to power an enormous festival. Children on both sides learned the version their parents had heard. One Azureward child found an old recording of an Amberward birthday party and mistook it for an emergency evacuation. The misunderstanding was never officially corrected.

Above the station's central junction, two ancient guardians watched over the divided communities.

They were known as the Astral Twins.

Dawn, the elder sister, glowed with the brilliant coral light of the warm star. She was fast, impatient, and preferred solving problems by approaching them at extremely high speed.

Dusk, her younger brother, carried the cold blue light of the other star. He was precise, reserved, and preferred solving problems from a comfortable distance.

Dawn could shove objects aside with a magnetic burst. Dusk could pull them toward him. They had once maintained the station’s balance together. Now the damaged machinery flung them into opposite chambers whenever they got close.

This did not stop them from arguing.

In fact, it gave them considerably more to argue about.

## The Great Rivalry

Both wards claimed to have invented the same soup. Amberward called it Solar Stew and served it boiling. Azureward called it Lunar Broth and served it chilled. Neither side cared to compare the recipes. Azureward put up a statue of Dusk holding a book he’d never read. When he asked why, the sculptor said it made him look intelligent. Dusk said he was already intelligent. The sculptor considered this proof that the statue was accurate.

The twins themselves communicated through an ancient station radio.

Most of their conversations began with an important technical matter and ended in a personal insult.

“Your magnetic stabilizer is drifting again,” said Dawn.

“It is within acceptable limits,” said Dusk.

“Your acceptable limits nearly threw me into a wall,” said Dawn.

“An unfortunate missed opportunity,” said Dusk.

When something broke, Dawn rushed in to carry people out. Dusk worked out which walls would buckle and held them up. Neither would admit how useful the other was.

## The First Crack

The trouble began in Amberward's central market.

Rook was repairing a heating pipe when his wrench flew to the ceiling. Every fork and saucepan in the market followed. A chef made one attempt to catch a ladle and watched it go by. “My kitchen!” he shouted at the ceiling.

Across the station, Neri caught a cabinet containing thirty years of research before the magnetic field could pull it through her laboratory door. Her instruments showed the central synchronizer failing.

She immediately contacted Amberward's engineering department.

Rook answered.

“This is Azureward Engineering. Your magnetic output is interfering with our stabilizers,” said Neri.

“Our output? Your side's been pulling our tools through the ceiling!” said Rook.

“That is physically unlikely,” said Neri.

“Would you like me to send you a flying saucepan?” asked Rook.

“Preferably not,” said Neri.

An alarm cut them off. A crack had opened in the junction. Both councils blamed the other, and neither had the equipment to repair it alone. Rook and Neri dug up the same access tunnel in old maintenance records.

They arrived at the sealed junction at almost the same moment.

Rook had brought a welding torch, three spare batteries, and a large hammer.

Neri had brought diagnostic instruments, precision tools, and a repair manual.

When the doors opened, they stared at each other.

“You're from Azureward,” said Rook.

“And you're holding a hammer,” said Neri.

“It's a multipurpose tool,” said Rook.

“It has one purpose,” said Neri.

“It's very good at it,” said Rook.

The station tilted. They fell through the doorway, and the barrier slammed shut behind them.

## The Missing Half

Rook suggested hitting the barrier. Neri explained what it had been built to withstand. When he suggested hitting it harder, she took his hammer away. They went looking for another way out.

At the station's center, they discovered a massive circular machine divided into coral and blue halves.

The original magnetic synchronizer.

Most of its control panels were dark.

But two terminals remained active.

Rook approached the coral terminal.

Neri approached the blue one.

Beneath each terminal sat half of an Opal. The half beneath Rook's hand was coral. Neri's was blue.

Neri wiped the dust from a diagram etched into the casing. Each half was meant to echo its pulse on the other side. If the two pulses arrived together, the crews would know the fields were in step.

Rook waited for the blue half to answer. It stayed dark.

Both terminals displayed incomplete instructions.

Rook's screen read:

“IN THE EVENT OF MAGNETIC INSTABILITY, DISCONNECT THE—”

The remaining text was missing.

Neri's screen read:

“—ONLY AFTER BOTH SIDES HAVE CONFIRMED ALIGNMENT.”

Neri laid the two sets of instructions side by side. A single terminal had never been enough to operate the machine.

“Why would anyone build a system that needs two people?” asked Rook.

“Because one person making a mistake could destroy the station,” said Neri.

“That's terribly inconvenient,” said Rook.

“It is called a safety feature,” said Neri.

They found a recording of the Great Separation. Through the crackle, a technician reported an incoming surge. Then two voices spoke at once, one from each ward, ordering their crews to disconnect. Both had tried to save the station. Their emergency measures worked, but the two systems had been running out of step ever since.

Rook sat down.

“So nobody started the disaster?” asked Rook.

“A magnetic surge did,” said Neri.

“And both sides tried to stop it?” asked Rook.

“Correct,” said Neri.

He considered this.

“That's disappointing,” said Rook.

“Why?” asked Neri.

“I had a very good speech prepared about how wrong you were,” said Rook.

Neri almost smiled.

Then the station shook again.

A warning appeared on both terminals.

STRUCTURAL FAILURE IMMINENT.

Their discovery had come just in time.

## Push and Pull

The old repair procedure needed the twins to align both poles at the same instant.

Rook contacted Dawn.

Neri contacted Dusk.

They arrived from opposite directions. The magnets threw Rook into a wall, dragged Neri across the room and sent a maintenance robot into a ventilation shaft. The twins landed on opposite sides of the junction.

“You pulled too hard!” said Dawn.

“You approached too quickly!” said Dusk.

“I always approach quickly!” said Dawn.

“That is the problem!” said Dusk.

Rook slowly stood.

“Are they always like this?” asked Rook.

Neri sighed.

“Apparently,” said Neri.

Dusk showed them how much further the fields drifted with each cycle. Dawn proposed forcing the poles together. Neri interrupted before either could demonstrate. The blueprints showed a rhythm: when one side pushed, the other had to pull. Every chamber's generator had to change together.

Rook stared at the diagram.

“So we need Amberward and Azureward to work together,” said Rook.

“Yes,” said Neri.

“At exactly the same time,” said Rook.

“Yes,” said Neri.

Rook looked toward the ceiling.

“We are going to die,” said Rook.

## The Worst Teamwork in History

One council member wanted the historical dispute settled before the crews worked together. Neri suggested settling it after they had prevented everyone from dying. Then the twins went on the radio and gave both councils the same message. Hearing Dawn and Dusk agree alarmed everyone more than the warnings had.

The repair crews gathered. For an hour they argued over how to synchronize the morning drum with the rise of a blue star.

The clock was discovered inside the central junction.

It had been functioning perfectly for seven hundred years.

Rook stared at it.

“We could have used this the whole time?” asked Rook.

“Apparently,” said Neri.

“I hate this station,” said Rook.

They reconnected the four chambers, repaired the generators and put a technician at each station. Dawn and Dusk took their places beside the synchronizer. Rook and Neri each took a terminal.

The entire station held its breath.

“All systems ready,” said Neri.

“Amberward ready!” said Rook.

“Magnetic fields stable,” said Dusk.

“Can we start already?” asked Dawn.

Neri began the countdown.

At one, both wards pulled their switches.

Energy rushed through the junction. Dawn and Dusk came within reach of one another. Then the old system screamed and threw them apart.

Warning lights filled the chamber.

ALIGNMENT FAILED.

Dawn struck a wall.

Dusk barely managed to stabilize himself.

Rook looked at the readings.

“What happened?” asked Rook.

Neri stared at the display.

“The timing was correct,” said Neri.

Dusk traced the repulsion on the display. The old synchronizer could no longer absorb it. They would have to redirect the force into the outer ring, using two controls at opposite ends of the chamber.

Dawn immediately volunteered.

So did Dusk.

They looked at each other.

“I'll handle the dangerous part,” said Dawn.

“Both parts are equally dangerous,” said Dusk.

“Then I'll handle mine better,” said Dawn.

“You are impossible,” said Dusk.

“And you're slow,” said Dawn.

For a moment, they smiled.

Then they returned to their positions.

This time, Dawn pushed when Dusk pulled. The central ring turned, coral and blue moving toward each other. Rook and Neri called adjustments to the crews over the radio. An Amberward technician accused an Azureward engineer of choosing the wrong valve. Azureward said the labels were backward. Both were right.

Then a coral pulse flashed beneath Rook's terminal. Neri saw it reflected in the blue half just as the blue pulse reached Rook's side.

“Together,” said Neri.

The readings steadied.

The central ring began glowing white.

Dawn and Dusk approached each other again.

The old magnetic system tried to repel them.

This time, the force flowed into the outer ring instead.

The violent pressure disappeared.

For the first time in seven hundred years, the twins stood together.

Dawn reached out.

Dusk took her hand.

The synchronizer completed its rotation.

And throughout the station, the alarms went silent.

## Under Both Stars

The passages opened. Warm light spilled into Azureward, and blue light reached Amberward. Neri found Rook beside the synchronizer and handed him a bowl of cold Lunar Broth. He tasted it and admitted it wasn’t terrible. She told him his stew was acceptable too.

“Acceptable?”

“High praise.”

At the window, Dawn told Dusk he had gotten taller. He pointed out that they were the same height. She said he looked shorter from across the station. He sighed, but when she leaned against him he rested his head on hers. Usually, this close to each other, they would already have flown to opposite sides of the junction. Neither was in a hurry to move.

That evening the crews cleared the junction for a celebration. Azureward had prepared a schedule. It lasted four minutes before Amberward began dancing. The music was too loud and the soup was the wrong temperature, but nobody shut the doors.

Dawn recognized the old station song and put out her hand. “Come on.”

“Where?” asked Dusk.

“We’re dancing.”

“I do not dance.”

“You used to.”

“That was seven hundred years ago.”

“Then you’ve had plenty of time to practice.”

He protested all the way to the floor. By the time they got there, he was laughing.
