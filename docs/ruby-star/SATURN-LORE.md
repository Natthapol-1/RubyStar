# Leave the Light On

## The Counting Game

On a lonely plateau above Saturn’s rings, the Frost Warden kept the landing pad open through blizzards. A cannon called the Hailer guarded the station. Most people knew the Warden by her heavy armor and single glowing eye. To Eda, she was Mother.

Eda waited for her in the observation room after shifts. She knew the uneven rhythm of her mother’s boots in the snow, but when two workers came in together she sometimes ran to the wrong one.

One evening, Eda decided to solve the problem.

She dragged a stool into the equipment room and climbed onto it with a pot of white paint.

Her mother was cleaning her helmet when Eda asked permission to decorate it.

The Warden reluctantly agreed.

Eda painted a large smiling mouth beneath the helmet's single eye.

The stool shifted halfway through, causing one corner of the smile to curve much higher than the other.

The Warden examined the result.

“It's crooked,” said the Warden.

“You're crooked when you walk,” said Eda.

“That's different,” said the Warden.

“No, it isn't,” said Eda.

Eda asked her to wear it to work so she could recognize her from the window. The Warden kept the smile. None of her soldiers dared comment.

When the station was quiet, two knocks on the observation room door meant hide-and-seek. The Warden counted to ten with her eyes shut and her helmet on. She always lost count around six.

“One, two, three, four, five, six...”

“Oh dear. Was that six or seven?”

Eda would giggle from wherever she was hiding.

“You're cheating!”

“Then I suppose I'll have to start again.”

Eda never hid quietly. When the Warden finally went to fetch her, Eda would jump out and shout, “You found me!” The Warden acted surprised every time.

## The Last Hiding Place

Snow swallowed the landing lights within minutes. The Warden was securing the pad when the alarm reported two intruders in the cargo passage. She sent the crew to the shelter. As she passed the observation room, Eda knocked twice.

At that exact moment, the radio crackled.

“Commander, they've reached the cargo crates. We can't hold them much longer,” said a guard.

“Give me ten,” said the Warden.

Eda heard the familiar words.

She smiled.

Her mother was counting.

She waited until six, just as she always did, then hurried down the stairs to find a hiding place.

Adults were running past her on the stairs. Eda found a gap behind a freight scale in the cargo passage, where nobody would think to look.

Meanwhile, the Warden reviewed the shelter roster.

Every crew member had been accounted for.

Eda's name was marked present.

The shelter supervisor had checked it automatically, knowing Eda was usually one of the first children to arrive.

The Warden saw the check beside Eda’s name. The intruders were closing in and no reinforcements could land.

“Hailer battery, fire over the cargo crates. Collapse the eastern passage,” said the Warden.

Behind the scale, Eda heard the Warden’s uneven stride. She rose from her hiding place, smiling.

Then the corridor filled with blue-white light.

The Warden saw a small figure rising behind the crates.

A little girl.

Her daughter.

For a fraction of a second, neither moved.

Then Eda smiled.

“You found me!”

The Warden threw out her hand.

An enormous frost shield erupted across the passage.

But the Hailer's shell was already falling.

The blast struck before the shield could close around Eda.

The cargo passage vanished beneath shattered ice and twisted metal.

The Warden ran forward, calling her daughter's name.

She found Eda beneath the wreckage.

The child was motionless.

Without hesitation, the Warden used her most powerful technique.

Absolute Zero.

The world around them stopped.

Falling shards hung in the air. Snowflakes remained suspended above the ground. The flames from the damaged machinery froze into strange, motionless shapes.

Even the sound of the alarms disappeared.

The Warden knelt beside her daughter.

Eda’s mouth was slightly open. The Warden waited for her to speak, then put a hand against her chest.

## What the Diamond Could Save

The Warden carried Eda to the medical room and used the Diamond to hold her body at the moment of the blast. Ilan, the station’s medic, tried everything he knew. When she eased the field for a fraction of a second, Eda’s heart did not beat. She froze it again.

The medic stood beside her for a long time.

Finally, he pulled a chair next to the examination table and sat down.

“I'm sorry,” said the medic.

The Warden did not answer.

She was staring at her daughter's face.

There was no blood now. The Diamond had preserved her so perfectly that she appeared to be sleeping.

The Warden searched the Diamond’s old records until she found a way to preserve a person’s memories and carry them into another living body. The transfer erased the identity already there. Eda could wake with her mother’s hands and face. The Warden would be gone.

She told the medic what she intended to do.

He listened without interrupting.

Then he asked a simple question.

“What will you tell her?” asked Ilan.

The Warden imagined Eda waking in an unfamiliar body and asking where her mother had gone. That was when she began to cry.

The medic reminded her that Eda would need someone to look after her.

The Warden asked him to stay.

He promised he would.

She went back to the cargo passage and recorded what the report showed: the shelter roster had been wrong, and she had given the order to fire. Then she sat for a while in the observation room with Eda’s blanket folded on her lap.

Finally, she returned to the medical room.

She knelt beside Eda and gently moved a strand of hair away from her forehead.

For one last moment, she loosened the preservation field just enough to speak to her daughter.

“I have to go out on a rescue.”

“When you wake up, go to our chair.”

“You can stay up until I get back.”

She kissed Eda's forehead.

Then she looked at the medic.

“When she opens her eyes, call her Eda.”

The medic nodded.

The Warden placed the Diamond between them.

The transfer began.

Light spread across the room.

When it faded, the child's body lay still.

And inside the Frost Warden's body, Eda opened her eyes.

## The Girl in the Armor

Eda nearly struck her head on a lamp when she sat up. She recognized an old scar across one knuckle. In the mirror above the basin she saw her mother’s face.

“Where's Mother?”

Ilan told Eda what the Warden had said before the transfer. She was going on a rescue, and Eda could wait in their chair. Eda knew how often her mother left to save someone. She asked when she would return. He could not answer.

Most of the crew evacuated to a nearby settlement. Eda stayed in the observation room, where her mother had told her to wait.

She found the old helmet in an equipment locker.

The crooked white smile was still painted beneath its single eye.

She put it on.

She wore it beside the landing lamp and waved at each light in the storm. Some were supply ships. Some were only lightning.

The medic returned regularly with food and supplies.

He worried about her living alone in the abandoned station, but Eda insisted that her mother knew where to find her.

One evening, he found her trying to repair a broken heating pipe.

She had frozen the leaking section solid using her mother's powers.

The ice was thick and perfectly shaped.

The medic was astonished.

Eda looked pleased with herself.

“I didn't know I could do that,” said Eda.

“Your mother was very good with ice,” said the medic.

Eda examined her hands.

“Maybe she left me a little of it,” said Eda.

The medic looked away.

She could call up a shield when frightened. She still knocked cups off tables because her arms were longer than she expected.

One afternoon, she tried squeezing behind the freight scale where she had hidden during their last game.

Her shoulders were too wide.

She sat on the floor and stared at the narrow gap.

For the first time, she wondered whether her mother would recognize her at all.

## The Recording

Eda began keeping a notebook of passing ships. Ilan brought supplies and tried to answer her questions about the rescue. Each visit he had less to say.

One evening he found her holding the old security recorder. She’d found a file labeled with her mother’s name and wanted to know if it was important.

“Is Mother coming home?”

The medic had kept her fed and the station safe. He had also let her wait for someone who would never return. He sat down beside her.

“There's something your mother wanted you to hear,” said Ilan.

He pressed play.

Someone adjusted a microphone. Eda heard her mother describe the storm, the roster, and the order to fire.

“I believed my daughter was inside the shelter.”

A long silence followed.

“I gave the order.”

A long silence. Then the Warden explained the transfer, and why Eda would wake in her body.

“Eda, if you're hearing this, I hope someone is sitting beside you.”

“I wanted to stay. More than anything, I wanted to stay.”

“But I could not bear to let your story end because of my mistake.”

“You'll have my hands. Perhaps you'll even have some of the things they remember how to do.”

“But you don't have to become me.”

“You can be whoever you want.”

The Warden paused.

“And please, don't spend your whole life waiting for me.”

The recording ended.

Eda looked down at her hands.

“So she's not coming back?” asked Eda.

The medic shook his head.

Eda waited for him to explain something else.

He didn't.

“But these are her hands,” said Eda.

“Yes,” said the medic.

“And I can make her shields,” said Eda.

“Yes,” said the medic.

Eda looked at the dark window.

Her mother's reflection stared back.

“Then where did she go?” asked Eda.

The medic had no answer. Eda pressed play and listened to the first few seconds, when her mother adjusted the microphone and cleared her throat. She played those seconds again.

“She always did that before talking on the radio.”

The medic nodded.

Eda held the recorder close to her chest.

She began crying.

## Leave the Light On

Snow battered the windows. Eda sat in her mother’s chair with the helmet on her lap and looked out at the lamp.

“I thought the Diamond made it work,” said Eda.

The medic followed her gaze.

“No. It's connected to the station's power supply,” said the medic.

He pointed toward an ordinary switch on the wall.

“Your mother installed it herself, years ago,” said the medic.

She remembered her mother doing repairs in a storm and asking why the lamp needed to be on if nobody was coming.

“You don't leave the light on because you know someone's coming,” she had said. “You leave it on so they can find their way if they do.”

For months Eda had believed the light would guide her mother home. She looked at the switch.

“Can we turn it off?” asked Eda.

The medic stood.

Before he reached the wall, Eda caught his sleeve.

“Wait.”

Her voice trembled.

“I've never turned it off before she came home.”

The medic sat back down. Eda remembered how her mother would set the helmet on the chair when the game was over and tell her it was time for bed.

“I think I want to sleep,” said Eda.

Eda turned off the lamp. In the dark window she saw her mother’s face, and the reflection moved when she did. She took off the helmet and set it on the chair. The medic brought her blanket. She recognized the stitching even though it was much too small.

“Ilan, can you stay until I fall asleep?”

He returned to the chair beside her.

“Of course,” said the medic.

After a little while, Eda spoke again.

“Could you count to ten?” asked Eda.

The medic closed his eyes.

“One, two, three, four, five, six...”

“Oh dear. Was that six or seven?”

Eda let out a small laugh.

“You're cheating.”

“Then I suppose I'll have to start again.”

He counted slowly.

This time, Eda did not run away to hide.

When he reached ten, she was already asleep.

## The Morning Light

In the morning, Ilan was asleep in his chair. Eda went to the window. A supply ship was nosing through the last of the snow, its navigation lights flickering. She recognized the settlement’s markings and turned the lamp on.

“Expecting someone?” asked the medic.

“No.” Eda took the old helmet under her arm and went down to meet the passengers. “But they might need help.”

And above her, the landing light continued to shine.
