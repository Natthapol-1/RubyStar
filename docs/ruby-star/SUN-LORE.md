# The Last Shift

## The First Winter

The people of the Sun fear the cold.

To an outsider, winter on the Sun is still unbearably hot. But its people are adapted to temperatures that would destroy most other living creatures. When the temperature drops to four thousand degrees, they wrap themselves in heavy thermal coats and gather around heating vents.

Their cities depend on enormous solar forges that distribute heat through underground pipelines.

One winter, a solar storm destroyed the pipelines supplying the lower districts.

Homes began cooling. Residents crowded into public heating stations. Children became too weak to walk. The city's emergency reserves would not last the night.

The Fiery King opened the Royal Solar Forge to the public, allowing families to sleep beside its furnaces.

But at midnight, the machinery began its mandatory six-hour cooling cycle. The forge could not safely operate indefinitely without clearing its ash and allowing its coolant systems to recover.

The lower districts had only two hours of heat remaining.

The King took his Ruby and activated the emergency override. He descended to the furnace floor, removed his royal cloak, and asked the workers if they would help him keep the fire burning.

Every worker volunteered.

Among them was Mara, a seventeen-year-old apprentice.

She worked beside the King at a coolant valve. When the valve burned through her protective glove, he tore a strip from his royal cloak and wrapped her injured hand.

Mara was embarrassed to find herself crying in front of the King.

He smiled.

“Go ahead. Nobody can hear you over the pumps.”

They worked for thirty-one hours without stopping.

When the last district reported that its heating had returned, the entire forge erupted in celebration.

The King became a hero. The workers became heroes. The city erected a statue of the King holding his Ruby triumphantly above the furnace.

For a time, everyone remembered what they had accomplished together.

## The Night That Never Ended

The following winter, the lower districts requested funding to replace their damaged heating pipelines.

The council rejected the proposal.

The Solar Forge had already demonstrated that it could compensate for the failing infrastructure. Repairs, the council argued, were expensive and could wait.

The King reluctantly authorized another extended shift.

Then another.

Soon, keeping the forge running beyond its mandatory shutdown became normal.

At first, the King personally visited the workers to request their help. He thanked them, ate beside them, and occasionally worked alongside them.

Eventually, he became too busy.

Requests became written orders. Written orders became an automatic bell announcing another extension.

Nobody remembered the last time the bell had announced the end of a shift.

One evening, Mara's young son appeared at the bridge outside the forge. He had made a paper crown at school and wanted to show it to his mother.

Mara saw him through the window.

Her shift had already been extended twice. She told the foreman she needed to leave.

The King refused to release her until another worker could take her position.

Nobody was available.

The bridge gate remained closed.

Her son waited until a neighbor finally took him home.

Mara's anger was not simply that the King had refused her request. She knew the forge could not be abandoned.

What hurt was that he had stopped asking whether there was another way.

He had once worked beside her to save the city.

Now he barely knew how long she had been working.

## The Little Lights

Without its scheduled cooling cycles, the Solar Forge began accumulating ash.

Strange glowing creatures appeared in the warm corners of the factory.

The workers called them Cinder Wisps.

Most were harmless. They drifted around the machinery like floating embers, occasionally gathering near people or hiding inside discarded tools.

The workers became fond of one particularly small wisp. They named it Button and gave it an old metal spoon to sleep in.

But the wisps multiplied.

One night, hundreds gathered inside the north coolant reservoir.

Their combined heat overwhelmed the cooling pumps. Alarms sounded throughout the forge.

The King personally helped carry coolant buckets to stabilize the system.

In the morning, he ordered an additional pump installed.

He also ordered the emergency cutoff welded open. From then on, the furnace crew could only stop the forge if he came down and turned his Ruby.

To the King, an uninterrupted forge meant that nobody would ever suffer through the first winter again.

To the workers, it meant that the machinery was becoming more dangerous, and they were no longer allowed to stop it.

Mara resigned from her position.

Some workers followed her. Most could not afford to leave.

One of those who stayed was Pell, a senior stoker whose sister lived in the lower districts.

Pell understood Mara's objections.

But he also understood what would happen to his sister's home if the furnace stopped producing heat.

He continued defending the King, even as his shifts grew longer.

## The Six-Hour Solution

Mara did not abandon the forge entirely.

She began meeting with former colleagues, engineers, and cooling technicians.

They studied old blueprints and discovered something the council had overlooked.

The forge's thermal reservoirs held an enormous amount of unused heat. If connected directly to the district pipelines, they could keep homes warm while the main furnace cooled.

The stored heat would not last forever.

But it could last six hours.

Exactly long enough for the forge's mandatory maintenance cycle.

Over three winters, the workers constructed an independent bypass through an abandoned ash tunnel.

They salvaged pipes from condemned houses, recovered broken machinery, and completed much of the work between their regular shifts.

The system was eventually finished.

There was one problem.

The bypass could not be safely tested under full pressure without authorization from a Ruby. The King's own Ruby was already controlling the main furnace. Removing it to operate the bypass would risk interrupting the city's heat supply.

The workers had designed a second authorization socket so that two Rubies could safely operate the systems independently.

But nobody else possessed one.

The King inspected their work several times. Each time, he found another component he wanted reinforced, another valve he wanted tested, or another unlikely failure he wanted investigated.

His concerns were not entirely unreasonable.

If the bypass failed during a shutdown, entire neighborhoods could lose their heat.

But the workers eventually realized there would never be enough evidence to make the King feel completely safe.

Mara confronted him.

“Tell me what we need to do before you'll let us try.”

“Prove that no house will lose heat.”

“We can't prove that without running it.”

“And if you're wrong?”

“Then we fix it.”

“You say that as if the people who freeze while you fix it don't matter.”

Mara had no easy answer.

She understood his fear. She had been there on the first winter too.

But every day they delayed, the exhausted workers and deteriorating machinery became another danger to the city.

She asked the King to set a date.

He never did.

## The Second Ruby

Years later, a challenger completed the Fiery King’s Celestial Challenge. The King awarded them a Ruby and kept his own at the forge. When the challenger returned to the Sun, they crossed the bridge to see where the King’s Ruby had kept the furnace alive.

Workers hurried between the pumps. Cinder Wisps gathered around the overheated machinery. The shift bell sounded, and nobody headed for the gate.

Mara saw the Ruby at the challenger’s side and took them down to the Ash Tunnel. She showed them the second socket, still bright where the fitters had polished it years ago. With the main furnace running on the King’s Ruby, they could finally try the bypass.

“Four minutes,” she said. “Then I close the valve.”

The challenger seated their Ruby in the socket. Mara opened the valves, and the pipes trembled as stored heat flowed toward the lower wards.

At the last house in Ward Four, Pell’s sister Lyra held her hands over a cold heating tap. A sputter moved through the pipe, and the old metal began to glow. Pell had been checking that tap after his shifts for years. This was the first time it had run on heat from the bypass.

At four minutes, Mara closed the valve. She wrote down the pressure. No leaks, and the temperature had held.

## The Last Shift

Mara brought the pressure readings to the north reservoir. The King was sitting beside a young technician named Tavi.

Tavi was Lyra's daughter. The King had been helping her read an unfamiliar gauge. He took the register from Mara and ran his finger down the column.

“Four minutes?” he asked.

“Four minutes,” said Mara.

“You need six hours.”

“Yes.”

He asked what would happen if the bypass failed halfway through. Mara showed him how the crew would restart the main furnace. They could do it, given enough warning.

“Who takes responsibility?” he asked.

“All of us,” said Mara. “The same people who've been keeping this place alive.”

The King was quiet for a moment. Then he turned to Tavi and asked how long she had been working.

“Third shift, Your Majesty.”

“And who approved that?”

“You did.”

The King found his signature in the shift register. He remembered Mara beside him that first winter, holding a strip of his cloak against her burned hand. That night, he had asked who would stay. Tavi had been at the reservoir for three shifts because he had signed a piece of paper.

He asked Tavi to show him the new pressure gauge. Then he took her place. “Go home. I'll take the first watch.”

Mara gave the shutdown order.

The King turned his Ruby to begin cooling the main furnace. In the Ash Tunnel, the challenger set their Ruby into the bypass socket again. This time Mara opened the valve for the full six hours.

The furnace slowed. For the first time in years, the workers could hear water moving through the pipes. At the reservoir, Tavi’s gauge dipped below the mark she’d drawn in grease pencil. The King put his hand on the restart lever.

From the Ash Tunnel, Mara called for the lower return valve to be opened a quarter turn. The fitter working it asked her to say it again. In the quiet, everyone could hear the answer. The needle dipped once more, then climbed back to the mark.

The King took his hand off the lever. He left the gauge to the crew and went to the speaking tube to listen for Ward Four. It crackled.

“It's warm,” said Pell from Ward Four.

The workers looked at each other. The bell hadn't rung to send anyone home in years.

Mara handed the bell rope to the challenger.

When the bell rang, someone picked up their lunch tin. Another worker asked if they should come back after supper.

“Tomorrow,” said Mara.

The King looked up from the gauge and listened to their footsteps crossing the bridge.

## Tomorrow

Six hours later the forge came back online. The council still owed the wards new pipes. This time, the crew brought the King a maintenance schedule with the pressure readings from the test. He pulled up a chair and asked them what they needed.

In the north reservoir, Button the Cinder Wisp curled up in its old spoon. The machinery was cool enough for it to sleep.

On the bridge, the old statue still bore its original inscription.

“Here the Fiery King stood alone between his people and the cold.”

Someone had put up a second plaque below it.

“He wasn't alone.”
